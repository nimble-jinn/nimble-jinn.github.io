In development

Farholm

A space-mercenary roguelite about a small crew you come to love, and the risk of losing them.

You run a mercenary company out of a mothership you own outright — no bank, no mortgage, no repo-man. The ship is home. You sign contracts, fly to far-flung systems, fight through missions with a handful of named operators, salvage what you can, pay your people their shares, and come back to Farholm.

Some of them don't come back. When an operator dies, they are gone forever — their bunk stays empty, their name goes to the memorial wall. Permadeath is a founding pillar, not a difficulty option.

Farholm

The name, and the place

Farholm is the game's title and also a place in it — the station you call home. It is a Norse-flavoured coinage: holm is a small island. Spoken aloud in English it sounds like "far home." Both readings are the game: a small island of warmth, far away, that you keep leaving and coming back to.

The station is an anchored deep-space home, the origin of every run, and it carries every service the company needs. It is deliberately one place, for now.

The universe

One persistent galaxy

There is one galaxy, and it outlives every run. You do not get a fresh universe each campaign — the company lives somewhere real. A run is an expedition: the company takes a contract and deploys to a theater of operations, the way real mercenary outfits deploy. When the contract ends, the galaxy is still there, and what you did in it is remembered. A looted vault stays looted. A unique character found once is never found twice.

Star systems are the map's nodes — a star with planets on orbit rings, moons, and stations. Within a system, locations are the places you can actually dock at: a station's interior, scattered sites on a planet's surface. What a location offers is its facilities — a market, a medical bay, a recruitment hall, a shipyard — and a system's services show on the map, so "is this worth docking at?" is answerable at a glance.

Civilisation has a gradient, and the gradient is gameplay. Large interstellar hubs are where people concentrate: deep recruitment pools, busy markets. The frontier is the opposite — scattered facilities at lonely stations, scarce but sometimes special.

The universe keeps a continuous calendar of star dates. Time advances when you travel and act; the world is older than the company and will outlive it. Costs only accrue during runs — the game never punishes you for not playing — but the world's clock is real, so the frontier you visited last month may hold someone new.

The loop

Between contracts

The loop has two modes: life between contracts, and life under one. Both happen in the same galaxy, on the same ship. What changes is the pressure.

Between contracts you can fly anywhere. Visit hubs to interview recruits — hiring only works in person, so you travel to meet people. Hunt the frontier for a rare specialist. Buy equipment where it is cheap. There is no combat and no experience grinding out of a run, and income comes only from contracts, so wandering cannot break the game's balance. It exists for immersion, preparation, and the pleasure of a lived-in galaxy.

The run

Signing on

A run begins by accepting a scenario — a campaign contract with a win condition, loss conditions, and escalating pressure. Every run carries at least a loose deadline.

At signing you pick your roster, and it locks. The crew you take is the bet; there are no mid-run swaps. The ship's life support caps how many berths you have.

The contract gives you a board of missions in the theater, and all of them are mandatory, in any order you like. They are generated easiest-to-hardest and each wears a difficulty meter, so the deep end is visible from day one and every easy round you clear tightens the noose. You win by clearing the whole board. The deadline ticks over everything.

Between missions the theater is open — travel to a rare facility, resupply, detour for repairs. Every hour is real. What you cannot do mid-run is take other work or hire crew. If a mission proves too much you can retreat, and it stays on the board waiting for you, or abandon the run entirely and accept the consequences.

Where it is decided

The stages

Each mission plays as a chain of stages — real-time-with-pause tactical fights where you command your operators individually on a 2D field: move, attack, hold, withdraw.

This is where people die.

The simulation is fully deterministic: the same seed and the same orders produce the same outcome, every time. That matters for fairness, and it matters for verification — a quiet engineering pillar that shapes how the whole game is built and tested.

The rarest reward

Finding people

Rarely, in the middle of a run, you may find someone — a survivor in a cryopod, a defector. They ride with you, stowed in the cryo facility. If you win the run, bringing them into the company is part of the settlement ceremony. Miss the chance and it is gone forever: the scarcity is the thrill.

It is the game's rarest and most intense reward, and it is not an item. It is a person.

Some of the detail here is still in design.

The economy

Money, honestly

One company wallet. Mission rewards, advances that get clawed back if you quit, and operator shares — each crew member takes a percentage of every mission they ride, bigger for veterans. Supplies are bought stock: ammunition, missiles, materials, fuel.

The economy's job is pressure. A company too broke to fuel a mission has decisions to make.

Tone

Touchstones

These are the works we actually cite when we make decisions.

  • Cowboy Bebop — the found-family crew, a captain who owns his ship, humour that coexists with grief.
  • FTL: Faster Than Light — the ship as a top-down cutaway you look into, room by room.
  • Aliens (1986) — the crew biomonitor: in battle, the interface is the ship's telemetry watching your people's suits. Functional, and a little tense.
  • Master of Orion 2 — clean, readable orbit-ring maps of star systems.

Everything visual beyond those references is deliberately open. Palette, rendering style, shape language, the look of the species — none of it is settled, and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

Farholm is in active development. There is no release date to announce yet, and we would rather say nothing than say something we have to walk back.
